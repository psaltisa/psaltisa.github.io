          <p class="card-text">
            We are seeking a motivated M.Sc. student to join the nuclear astrophysics group at Saint Mary's University, starting in September 2027.
          </p>

          <p class="card-text">
           Our group investigates how nuclear reactions shape the production of elements in stars and stellar explosions. We combine experiments with stable and radioactive ion beams, nuclear reaction modelling, computational astrophysics and astronomical observations to understand environments such as supernovae and classical novae.
          </p>

          <h6 class="card-title mt-4">Research opportunities</h6>

          <p class="card-text">
            The successful candidate will be able to contribute to projects in several areas, including:
          </p>

          <ul class="card-text">
            <li><strong>Experimental nuclear astrophysics:</strong> Connecting nucleosynthesis models to measurements of astrophysically important nuclear reactions at facilities such as TRIUMF (Canada), Argonne National Laboratory (USA) and RIKEN (Japan).</li>
            <li><strong>Nuclear reaction rates and uncertainties:</strong> Studying how uncertainties in nuclear properties affect nucleosynthesis predictions and identifying priorities for future experimental campaigns.</li>
            <li><strong>Nucleosynthesis in stellar explosions:</strong> Modelling the production of heavy elements in supernovae and neutrino-driven outflows, and investigating the origin of abundance patterns observed in metal-poor stars.</li>
            <li><strong>Presolar grains and stellar nucleosynthesis:</strong> Using isotopic signatures preserved in stardust grains to investigate the conditions and nuclear reactions responsible for element production.</li>
          </ul>

          <p class="card-text">
            Projects will be developed in consultation with the successful candidate, taking into account their interests and background.
          </p>

The carbon in your DNA, the oxygen you are breathing, and the indium in the computer screen you are using right now—all were forged during the lives and explosive deaths of stars.
Our research seeks to answer a fundamental question: **“What is the origin of the chemical elements?”** The rarest elements, such as gold, silver, and platinum, are created in the universe’s most violent explosions, including supernovae and neutron star mergers.
We aim to determine precisely what comes out of these extreme environments, using a combination of experimental, theoretical, and observational approaches.

We collaborate with researchers worldwide, proposing and conducting experiments at leading nuclear physics facilities, including [TRIUMF](https://triumf.ca) (Canada), [RIKEN](https://www.nishina.riken.jp/ribf/) (Japan), [GSI](https://gsi.de/en/) (Germany), [FRIB](https://frib.msu.edu/), [Argonne National Lab](https://www.anl.gov/phy-0), and [TUNL](https://tunl.duke.edu/)  (United States).
These experiments allow us to recreate the nuclear processes that happen in the most exotic stellar explosions in the universe.

Science should be accessible to everyone.
Our group engages with the public through lectures, hands-on workshops for students, and collaborations with science festivals both in Canada and internationally.
Through these initiatives, we aim to inspire curiosity about the universe and bring the story of the stars closer to all audiences.
